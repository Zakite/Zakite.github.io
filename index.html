Jibrin

Minerals remember how they were made.Snacks also remember how they were made.

Snack loving PhD candidate studying how biology steers crystallization, from ion-regulated molecular assembly to mesocrystalline organization.

I am a PhD candidate in Earth and Planetary Sciences at Rutgers University, working with Shaunna Morrison. My committee includes Nathan Yee, Vikas Nanda, Paul Falkowski, and Richard Remsing.

My research asks how life enters the crystallization pathway and changes what forms. My master's work showed how microbial metabolism sequentially opened and closed pathways to calcite, struvite, and guanine monohydrate, each mineral altering the chemistry inherited by the next. The guanine is the part that should not have happened: it crystallizes from solutions at pH 0 to 3, and these plates grew at pH 8, five orders of magnitude away from where the mineral has any business forming. My PhD work follows that unexplained influence from solution chemistry through to the organized solid.

The goal is to treat that influence as a measurable continuum rather than a label. Minerals are not simply abiotic or biogenic. Metabolism, interfaces, precursor pathways, crystal chemistry, and structural organization each record a further degree of influence, and they can be ranked: a metastable phase held past the point where it should have converted is a stronger claim than the equilibrium polymorph that would have precipitated anyway, and a lattice aligned across an entire tissue is stronger still. Building that ranking is also what makes these records legible in samples where the organism itself is long gone.

From solution to organized solid.

My research follows biological influence through the whole pathway, beginning with the chemistry that decides which phases can precipitate, moving through ion-regulated assembly and crystallization by particle attachment, and ending in the unit-cell deviations, preferred orientations, and hierarchical structures preserved in the final material.

I

Precursors and pathway selection

How metabolism, ion identity, and hydration structure decide which mineralization pathways stay open before a mature crystal exists. I use guanine and uric acid to follow precursor formation, aggregation, particle attachment, ripening, and polymorph selection across prebiotic, functional, and pathological settings.

Before a crystal exists
II

Crystal-chemical deviation

Whether formation in a living system drives a mineral away from the structural behavior expected of abiotic chemistry alone. I am building comparative datasets for carbonates and phosphates to test whether composition, unit-cell geometry, vibrational disorder, and inherited precursor structure vary systematically with the degree of influence.

Within the lattice
III

Crystallographic organization

How influence is expressed between crystals rather than within them: preferred orientation, constrained misorientation, mesocrystalline superstructure, and hierarchical architecture. Because orientation is inherited as particles attach, the misorientation distribution is a record of the assembly itself. I ask whether its degree and complexity can separate chemically driven precipitation from tightly controlled mineral formation.

Crystallization by particle attachment.

Classical models grow a crystal one monomer at a time. The biomineralization literature shows a second route: ions cluster into higher-order species, prenucleation clusters, droplets, amorphous particles, and even whole nanocrystals, that attach to the growing solid. My work on guanine and urate precursors sits on these upper pathways, where the particle that attaches is already partly ordered before it arrives.
